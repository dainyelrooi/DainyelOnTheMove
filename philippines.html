The beginning · Cebu City

The bucket-list trip, finally.

Here it is: the Philippines. After my earlier adventures in Southeast Asia, this was the country sitting right at the top of my list. Why? Honestly, I did not have a very good answer. I had a picture of it in my head, but almost no idea what being there would actually feel like. Then I arrived. Wow.

I had just been travelling alone in Thailand, so my journey began with a flight from Chiang Mai to Manila and another to Cebu City. This time, I would be exploring with friends. Different energy, different stories, and a lot less peace and quiet. Looking back, it became one of the biggest adventures I have had—and one I remember with a ridiculous amount of happiness.

But first: getting everyone together. I reached our hotel about three hours before the rest of the group arrived. A few of the boys were already in Cebu and had done some exploring. The city? Well… mostly its nightlife. Conveniently, that meant they already knew where to take us for dinner and a drink.

Those first days were more about eating, bars and laughing at each other than serious sightseeing. Cebu is a big city, but for our trip it worked best as a starting point. We were ready to get to the islands. After three nights out, though, we had already gathered enough stories for a film. Think The Hangover, Cebu edition. What happens in the Philippines stays in the Philippines. Some of it, anyway.

Did you know? · The bigger picture

7,641 islands. One very small sample.

The Philippines is an archipelago of 7,641 islands, grouped into Luzon, the Visayas and Mindanao. Our journey only scratched the surface.

Source: Philippine Embassy in The Hague ↗

How our trip unfolded

Cebu → Oslob → Panglao/Bohol → Cebu → Boracay, via Malay → El Nido → Puerto Princesa → Manila.

We took the bus south to Oslob, boats to Panglao and back to Cebu, then flights towards Boracay and El Nido. After El Nido, a public bus took us to Puerto Princesa for our flight to Manila.

Oslob · South along Cebu

An early alarm. A very big fish.

The bus to Oslob was supposed to take around five hours. Supposed to. Seven or eight hours later, we finally arrived, and a long bus ride does not become more enjoyable with a hangover. Our hostel was a welcome landing: friendly people, a game of pool, some conversation and an evening to recover.

We needed the rest. Our alarm went off at 5:30 the next morning because we were going swimming with whale sharks. Yes, whale sharks. I had no idea what to expect, but an enormous crowd at six in the morning was not it. It felt like a thousand or two thousand people were already waiting. The crowd was much bigger than I had imagined.

Had I known how touristy it would be, I would have thought harder about joining. We went ahead, and the size of the animals was unbelievable. They swam right beside us, close enough that I felt one brush against me. It was one of those moments when your brain needs a second to catch up with what is happening.

Did you know? · Beneath the surface

Meet the world’s largest fish.

It is the world’s largest fish and a filter feeder, eating tiny prey such as plankton and fish eggs. Researchers can also recognise individual whale sharks by their spot patterns.

NOAA Fisheries ↗ · LAMAVE research ↗

A little context for this memory

Oslob’s encounters involve feeding, which LAMAVE’s research links to changes in whale shark behaviour. The encounter rules prohibit touching or riding the animals. The contact I remember is not something to seek out: give wildlife space, never chase it, and read about the encounter guidelines before deciding whether a tour fits the way you want to travel.

And that was only the beginning of the day. Our guide took us to Tumalog Falls, where the water felt freezing. Outside, it was around 35 degrees; in the pool, I suddenly had rather less to say about the heat. Brrr. The huge curtain of water, the green surroundings and all of us together made it another day I will not forget.

There was barely time to stop. Once the tour finished, we were off to the boat for the next island. Dry off, pack up, move on. We were really doing this.

Panglao & Bohol · Taking the long way

The road was half the adventure.

Our next base was Panglao, in Bohol province. We rented scooters on arrival and headed to Glamping Alona. In practice, we mostly slept there. The days were for exploring, and in the evenings we ended up playing pool at Escape Alona Beach Bar. A new island, a familiar routine.

One thing I want to be clear about: being able to rent a scooter easily does not mean you are allowed or insured to ride it. Many vehicles sold as “scooters” are motorcycles. Check the vehicle category, your licence, any International Driving Permit or translation requirements, and the terms of your insurance before renting. Our group had riding experience, but experience does not replace the right licence. NetherlandsWorldwide explains what an IDP does; check the Philippines-specific licence information too.

The next early start took us towards the Chocolate Hills. Our scooter journey took about four hours, and I would happily relive the scenery along that ride. Quiet stretches of road, green countryside, villages and little glimpses of everyday life: getting there became a huge part of the memory.

There was also a moment of perspective. Coming from Europe, I realised how comfortable my own life was. At the same time, I found myself wondering what I would give up to live somewhere surrounded by this much nature. I was seeing it as a visitor, of course, with a very different set of worries from someone making a life there. Still, the thought stayed with me.

Did you know? · Getting your bearings

Panglao: an island within Bohol province.

Panglao is an island in Bohol province, connected by bridges to the larger island of Bohol. Staying near Alona Beach puts you on Panglao; the Chocolate Hills are inland on Bohol.

Source: Bohol Provincial Tourism Office ↗

And the hills themselves? A landscape that almost looks invented: rounded mounds rolling away into the distance. Add the Bilar man-made forest and the roads between the stops, and it was a lot more than one viewpoint. I loved that whole day.

Did you know? · Why “chocolate”?

The colour changes with the season.

The UNESCO Tentative List description records about 1,776 Chocolate Hills. Their grassy covering turns brown in the dry season, giving them their name. The hills are natural limestone formations shaped by erosion.

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre ↗
Did you know? · Beyond the viewpoint

Bohol became the Philippines’ first UNESCO Global Geopark.

The designation came in 2023. The Chocolate Hills are part of its wider geological story, with limestone formed from ancient coral reefs. The roadside mahogany forest around Bilar and Loboc is different: a planted forest stretching for about two kilometres.

UNESCO Global Geoparks ↗ · Bohol Tourism ↗

But our next connection was waiting, so eventually we had to leave. The boat took us back to… Cebu City. We had missed it so much that we simply had to return. All right, not quite. Going through Cebu made sense for our route. And since we were there, we might as well surprise the people at the bars again.

The next day, we were heading for what felt like paradise. There was just one small obstacle: a propeller plane. My favourite. And by favourite, I mean one of my biggest fears. Spoiler: I survived.

Boracay · Five days by the water

A roof terrace, a seafood feast, a sunset.

After travelling via Malay, we reached Boracay by boat and took a little tricycle from the harbour. My first impression changed as we moved across the island. Everyday streets gave way to the busy beach side, and suddenly there it was: white sand, incredibly blue water, greenery and small boats scattered across the sea. Touristy? Absolutely. Beautiful? Also absolutely.

We stayed five days at Mayuma Beach Villa. There were eight of us there, spread across three floors with bedrooms, living spaces, bathrooms and kitchens. It felt enormous for our group. Two members of staff helped us during the stay, and then there was the roof terrace. Honestly, that terrace deserves its own paragraph.

We watched sunsets from up there, with boats out on the water and the light changing over the beach. Those are the evenings I remember very clearly. The later nights? Some are a little less sharp. The clubs were around a twenty-minute walk from our villa, and we made good use of that.

We ate out together, but some of our best meals started at the market. We bought seafood—huge prawns, oysters, fish and other shellfish—and turned it into our own meal with several courses. A group of friends, a kitchen and far too much enthusiasm: it was a very good combination.

Did you know? · That long strip of white

White Beach stretches for around four kilometres.

Boracay sits just off the northwestern tip of Panay. White Beach faces west, while Bulabog Beach is on the island’s eastern side—two quite different coastal settings on one small island.

Source: Philippine Department of Tourism ↗
Boracay · Travel films

The coast, our roof terrace and a morning at the seafood market.

A beach seller also talked us into an island tour. We snorkelled, jumped from cliffs and visited beaches we would probably never have reached on our own. It was genuinely a lot of fun. There is something special about going out on a boat and seeing the coast from another angle, rather than always looking towards the water from the shore.

Eventually, the Boracay chapter had to end. The adventure, fortunately, did not. Another flight, another propeller plane, another opportunity for me to pretend I was completely relaxed. Next stop: El Nido.

El Nido · Above the coast, between the islands

The kind of blue you keep trying to photograph.

In El Nido we rented another villa, a little smaller this time, and hired scooters again. We had hardly settled into this new chapter before we were off to the El Nido Zipline. Doing that together, with the sea and islands below, was exactly the sort of activity that made this trip feel like an adventure.

In the evenings, we went to Amigos. The nightlife was another part of El Nido for us, alongside the views and excursions. Somehow, we kept finding enough energy for both.

Then came our island-hopping day: Tour A. We visited beautiful places, went kayaking and spent a lot of time laughing. I also had the chance to fly my drone. Getting those shots was exciting, but sending it out over that landscape made me nervous too. The photographs and films bring back both feelings: the view in front of me and the concentration behind the camera.

What I loved was the change of scale. From the boat, the cliffs looked huge. From a kayak, the water felt close and everything slowed down. From above, the beaches and boats became tiny details in a much bigger landscape. I could point a camera in almost any direction and still feel as if it was not quite capturing what being there was like.

Did you know? · A living landscape

El Nido’s scenery is part of a protected area.

The El Nido Managed Resource Protected Area includes much more than dramatic rock: its ecosystems include forests, caves, coral reefs, seagrass and mangroves. The landscape above the water is only part of the picture.

Source: DENR Biodiversity Management Bureau ↗
El Nido · Travel films

A zipline ride, the islands from above and a moment in the kayak.

El Nido · When the plan stopped working

You can plan a route. You cannot plan the weather.

Then a typhoon crossed the Philippines, and our plans changed. We had wanted to continue to Moalboal and a few other islands, but instead we were stuck in El Nido. The mood was not always brilliant. After so many days of moving and doing things, suddenly being unable to continue was frustrating.

I still tried to find something to enjoy. My best friend and I did a climbing course, and I went out on my own one evening. The canopy walk, the bay under low cloud and those wet, green views became another side of the trip. This is how that chapter unfolded for us; weather warnings and local closures should always decide which activities are possible.

Eventually, we changed the route and aimed for Manila for the final part of the holiday. We took a public bus from El Nido to Puerto Princesa and flew from there. It was not the ending we had planned, but the trip was still giving us new experiences. They just looked rather different from the ones on our original list.

Did you know? · Island weather

A storm can change more than one day’s plans.

PAGASA reports an average of about 20 tropical cyclones entering the Philippine Area of Responsibility each year; around eight or nine cross the Philippines. The area of responsibility is much larger than the country, so those figures do not mean 20 landfalls. July to October is the peak season.

Source: PAGASA ↗
Manila · One last change of scene

From limestone cliffs to the concrete jungle.

After the islands, Manila felt like stepping into another world. We had swapped all that nature for the concrete jungle. We went to a boxing show featuring performers of short stature, spent evenings out together and took things fairly slowly during the day. I also finally got the chest tattoo I had wanted for a long time. A souvenir that was definitely not going in my suitcase.

I love travelling with a group, but I also need small breaks from all the noise and activity. That does not mean I am not enjoying myself. Sometimes, a few hours walking around alone are enough to feel like myself again. In Manila, I made room for those little escapes and some sightseeing, including the gate of Fort Santiago.

Those walks gave me a different way to remember the city: church courtyards, street food, old trees and everyday scenes between the bigger sights. After so much time together, it was good to notice things at my own pace, then meet the others again with a little more energy.

Did you know? · Behind the gate

Fort Santiago has been rebuilt more than once.

The Spanish fort dates to 1571, with its stone form built between 1589 and 1592. Its gate was destroyed during the Second World War and restored in the 1980s. The fort also held José Rizal as a prisoner before his execution.

Intramuros: the gate ↗ · Museo ni Rizal ↗

Looking back · The part you take home

Beautiful, busy, and completely unforgettable.

Then it was time for the Netherlands again. The flight home took around fifteen hours, and I think I slept through thirteen of them. After that trip, apparently even I had finally run out of energy.

There is one part I have not said much about yet: the food. Normally, I am the person who wants to try local dishes, and you will not often find me choosing a fast-food chain. The Philippines somehow saw rather more of that version of me. KFC, McDonald’s, Burger King, Jollibee… there were plenty of choices, and we did not exactly make our healthiest decisions.

I did try local food too. Some of the dishes I ate had sweeter flavours than I expected—especially coming straight from Thailand. I am not saying every Filipino dish tastes sweet, or that our choices represented the whole country’s cooking. It was simply a different flavour profile from the one I had just got used to, and I preferred some meals more than others. The market seafood we cooked together was its own very happy chapter.

Did you know? · A familiar red sign

Jollibee’s story started with ice cream.

The family business began selling ice cream in Manila in 1975. Three years later, it moved towards hot meals and the Jollibee brand. One of the fast-food names we kept seeing had a very local beginning.

Source: Jollibee Group’s history ↗

What stayed with me most was the welcome. We met friendly, hospitable people all along the way, and being able to communicate easily in English helped us connect. The landscapes were extraordinary, but it was the combination of people, nature, activities and time with friends that made the trip so special.

Did you know? · A multilingual country

Filipino and English are official languages.

Filipino developed from a Tagalog base. Alongside Filipino and English, the constitution recognises regional languages as auxiliary official languages in their regions—a small reminder that one country can hold many different voices.

1987 Constitution, Article XIV ↗ · 1937 national-language order ↗

What stayed with me

I went to the Philippines with a bucket-list idea and came home with a very real collection of memories: a bus journey that would not end, freezing waterfall water, long island roads, rooftop sunsets, nervous flights and a lot of laughter. It was beautiful, busy and occasionally frustrating. It was also an adventure I am incredibly glad we shared.

Some trips give you the view you imagined. This one gave me a story I could never have planned.
